Five million variants. One hypothesis.

A disease can be too uncommon for a market, a clinical trial or even a nearby specialist. It should not be too uncommon for the world’s knowledge to gather around one patient.

By Prabal Banerjee (@prabalbanerjee) and Codex (GPT‑5.6‑sol)

Imagine that a child’s illness has been seen only a handful of times—or perhaps never in exactly the same form. There may be no specialist in their city, no established treatment and no pharmaceutical company for whom the numbers make commercial sense. Yet somewhere in the world may already exist every fragment needed to understand what went wrong.

One laboratory has seen a similar genetic change. A database knows that it is almost absent from healthy populations. A paper describes what the affected protein does. Another family has reported overlapping symptoms. A structural model shows the part of the protein that may have broken. Rare-disease medicine often fails not because nothing is known, but because the pieces are scattered across institutions, countries and technical languages.

The promise of AI is not that it knows the answer. It is that no useful fragment of knowledge has to remain alone.

More than 7,000 rare diseases collectively affect over 300 million people, most beginning in childhood. Each condition is uncommon; the underlying problem is not. Millions of families are trying to assemble explanations from evidence too dispersed for any one clinician, researcher or patient group to continuously track.

That is beginning to change. Sequencing can turn a person’s biology into data that software can search. Public databases record which genetic changes have appeared in healthy populations and which have been linked to disease. Shared vocabularies let symptoms described in different hospitals be compared. AlphaFold offers a predicted view of proteins that once required years of laboratory work to see. AI can move between these layers, translate their specialist languages and help preserve the logic of an investigation.

In Australia, AI entrepreneur Paul Conyngham used AI to navigate unfamiliar genomics after his dog Rosie’s aggressive cancer persisted through conventional treatment. He then worked with sequencing facilities and university researchers to pursue a treatment built around her tumour’s mutations. The story made international news not because a chatbot cured cancer—the science and care came from specialists—but because it showed what becomes possible when sophisticated science is navigable beyond the small circle already fluent in it.

The result could be larger than faster diagnosis. Families could become active research participants. Patient groups could organise knowledge that no market has an incentive to collect. Engineers could build tools around neglected conditions. Researchers could expose well-bounded questions to thousands of capable people. When knowledge can gather around one patient, rarity no longer has to mean isolation.

The hackathon described here was one small test of that future. A controlled genome from a real rare-disease case contained 5,012,204 candidate differences. The task was to use today’s open scientific infrastructure to reduce those five million possibilities to one hypothesis worth testing.

01 — The benchmarkA real genome, not a toy problem

The Rare Disease, Real Kid: MVA Hackathon invited public participants to analyse a controlled-access genome and rank the variant pair most likely to cause a child’s illness. A genome is not a clean program with one obvious bug. Every person carries millions of differences, nearly all harmless; some matter only when inherited in a particular combination, in a particular gene, against a particular clinical history.

The task was therefore not simply to find something unusual. It was to distinguish two consequential letters from millions of normal ones, connect them to the same biological mechanism, and assemble enough independent evidence to justify the claim. The signal can be one letter, while a biologically elegant story can still be wrong.

The analysis followed two constraints. Clinical features (the phenotype) and genetic variants (the genotype) were examined separately before they were allowed to meet. And AI output never counted as biological evidence. AI could translate a term, write a parser, propose a check or locate a database; every material claim still had to survive a primary source or a calculation on the supplied data.

02 — Two searchesRead the symptoms. Read the genome.

The safest way to fool yourself is to find an interesting mutation and retrofit the symptoms around it. We ran the search in both directions instead.

Phenotype first: clinical observations were translated into Human Phenotype Ontology terms. A small Python program measured semantic similarity between that set and the known phenotype profile of each disease gene. This is more useful than keyword matching: “poor growth” and a more specific growth abnormality can be biologically close even when the words differ.

Genotype first: the variant call format (VCF) file—a list of differences from a reference genome—was inventoried, quality checked, and annotated locally. We retained variants likely to alter a protein or essential splice site, then asked whether they were technically convincing, rare in the general population, compatible with an inheritance model, and supported by clinical databases.

03 — The funnelFive million is manageable when every filter has a reason

A language model did not “read the genome.” Ordinary bioinformatics tools performed the deterministic work; AI helped assemble, inspect and challenge the workflow around them.

The privacy boundary mattered. The clinical document, raw reads, VCF, and patient-level working tables never entered the public repository. Annotation databases were downloaded locally. Coordinates were not pasted into public web tools. Only reusable code, aggregate counts, methods, and the final competition answer were published.

04 — The hypothesisCould both copies of a checkpoint gene be impaired?

The two searches converged on BUB1B, a gene involved in the machinery that checks whether chromosomes are correctly attached before a cell divides. When both copies are sufficiently impaired, cells can end up with the wrong number of chromosomes. That mechanism is associated with mosaic variegated aneuploidy syndrome 1 and cancer predisposition.

chr15:40220612 T→G · p.Asn1002Lys · a missense change in the C-terminal region
Evidence
High-quality heterozygous call; population singleton; several damaging predictions; high-confidence AlphaFold region. Retained as a variant of uncertain significance (VUS), not promoted to “pathogenic.”

The proposed model was compound heterozygosity: a different variant on each copy of BUB1B. Because the dataset contained only the child, we could not establish whether the variants were on opposite chromosomes or determine which parent contributed either allele. The two variants were 10.9 kilobases apart—too far for ordinary short reads to phase confidently.

The line we would not cross

This is a strong competition hypothesis, not a diagnosis. The second allele remains uncertain, phase is unresolved, structural variants were not comprehensively excluded, and parental testing would be the next decisive experiment.

05 — Try to break itA good story is not enough

Once BUB1B looked compelling, the task changed from finding support to finding failure. We checked the reference bases against GRCh38, normalized the variants, verified transcript and protein notation, compared population frequency and homozygote counts, examined every high-confidence clinical match, and reviewed other possible same-gene pairs.

Plausible alternatives were inspected rather than quietly discarded. A nearby cluster in another recessive disease gene looked more like variants travelling together on one chromosome than two independent hits. Several known pathogenic variants in unrelated genes were better interpreted as carrier or incidental findings because their inheritance and phenotypes did not explain the whole case.

AlphaFold supplied a useful final check: the uncertain amino acid sat in a high-confidence, tightly packed region of the protein. That made disruption plausible. It did not turn a VUS into a pathogenic variant. A predicted structure is still a model; a model is not an experiment.

The useful unit of AI assistance was not an answer. It was the next falsifiable check.

06 — What changedThe frontier moved from knowing to coordinating

The important result is not that a non-specialist can imitate a geneticist. It is that rare-disease work is becoming modular. Population evidence, clinical classifications, phenotype ontologies, consequence annotation and protein models are separate expert achievements that can now be composed into a reproducible investigation.

Open databases supplied population and clinical evidence. Standard command-line tools performed reproducible genomics. AlphaFold supplied structural context. AI translated concepts, drafted code, caught inconsistencies and maintained a map of the investigation. Experts and laboratories remain responsible for validation and every decision that touches a patient.

That modularity changes who can contribute. Families can arrive at specialist appointments with better questions. Patient groups can organise neglected evidence. Software engineers can improve analysis infrastructure. Researchers can expose bounded problems to a much larger surface area of motivated attention. A condition may be rare to a health system yet the most important problem in the world to one family.

This can become a movement if participation is structured well: controlled data, reproducible code, explicit uncertainty, expert review and clear hand-offs from computation to experiment. The aim is not to dissolve expertise. It is to let more effort reach expertise in a form that experts can actually use.

07 — The industry after averagesFrom blockbuster drugs to programmable care

Pharma was built around averages: find a molecule that helps enough people with the same diagnosis, manufacture it at enormous scale, and accept that many patients will not respond. Genomics makes disease more granular. AI makes that granularity computable. Programmable platforms—RNA, gene editing, engineered proteins and cells—could eventually make the treatment itself more adaptable.

The near future will not be a retail website that safely ships bespoke medicines overnight. The hard parts do not disappear: causal inference, toxicity, delivery, trials, manufacturing quality, consent, regulation, reimbursement and long-term monitoring. Rare-disease families have already paid dearly for technological promises that outran evidence.

But the economic unit can change. Today, a one-patient therapy is exceptional. Tomorrow, the repeatable product may be the validated process: sequence, interpret, design, manufacture and monitor—while the molecular payload changes for each patient. Pharma then becomes partly a software-and-platform industry, hospitals become data-generating research partners, and personal health records become active models rather than passive archives.

The sequence matters. First, we make the impossible investigable. Then we make the investigable treatable. The first visible change is therefore not a miracle drug, but millions of people becoming capable of attempting questions that once required permission simply to understand.

The future begins when no disease is too rare to deserve the world’s attention.
